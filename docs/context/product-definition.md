## 1. Our product is (competitor) for (target user)

**Branch is Granola for multidisciplinary teams developing new ideas together.**

More specifically: **Branch turns a team's live conversation into a shared, editable map of ideas, assumptions and constraints, helping people see where their reasoning connects, diverges and develops into new possibilities.**

Pitch line: **See how your team thinks together.**

Granola supplies a recognizable, low-effort meeting-capture reference. Mapify is a visual-output reference. These are positioning analogies, not claims that Branch reproduces every feature of either product.

“Granola for teams” alone is too broad: Granola already markets to product teams. In the lecture's framework, this is an existing product category aimed at a more specific, potentially underserved workflow: collaborative concept development across disciplines. A new demographic market has not been established.

The live map is a working artifact during the conversation. AI-generated nodes and connections can appear automatically as clearly marked proposals within the agreed session audience. They become accepted relationships or agreed decisions only through explicit participant review. This distinction preserves flow without making AI an authority on what the group believes.

## 2. Our underserved community

**Start with small multidisciplinary design and engineering teams doing early concept development.** Their work involves open questions, competing constraints and several plausible directions. Product discovery teams and design studios are practical recruiting contexts; architecture and research teams are adjacent audiences to test later.

The need to test is: **“We capture the meeting, but lose how different viewpoints changed the idea and which assumptions remain unresolved.”** Examples include repeated debates, forgotten alternatives, apparent agreement masking different interpretations and ideas whose relevance becomes clear only later.

This is a specific professional workflow, not a product for everyone or a community defined by software expertise.

- **Initial adopter:** a design lead, workshop facilitator or technical lead who regularly brings disciplines together. They may become a power user of facilitation and review.
- **Everyday participants:** designers, engineers, researchers and other contributors. They should be able to join, talk, glance at the map and correct a suggestion without learning graph notation.
- **Experience principle:** frequent collaboration, casual operation. No required manual mapping, constant node approval or new habit of narrating every thought.

“Underserved” remains a hypothesis. Test whether these teams are dissatisfied with their current meeting notes, whiteboards and facilitation methods, and whether Branch improves a real session enough to earn repeat use.

## 3. Counter-positioning and differentiation

**Branch prioritizes creative discovery before commitment. It helps teams explore the possibilities inside a conversation and choose what to develop together.**

Positioning statement: **Branch helps multidisciplinary teams discover what they could create together. Its live map reveals possible tensions, connects complementary ideas, and keeps alternative directions open until the team is ready to decide.**

### The competitive distinction

[Granola's product positioning](https://www.granola.ai/use-cases/product) emphasizes shipping faster, turning meetings into PRDs, tickets and decisions, and reducing follow-up administration. It also offers cross-meeting insight discovery and research synthesis. Therefore, describe a difference in priorities and default experience; do not claim Granola only produces to-dos or cannot support creativity. Checked 18 October 2026.

Branch's chosen moment is **before a team knows which direction deserves execution**. The opportunity to test is premature closure: a discussion may produce a tidy plan while leaving incompatible assumptions or promising combinations unexplored. This is a workflow hypothesis, not evidence that Granola causes that problem.

| Product choice | Branch's proposed default |
|---|---|
| What receives attention | Meaningful tensions, complementary contributions and open questions |
| Main artifact | A shared, editable map with multiple possible directions and their evidence |
| AI's role | Make relationships inspectable and propose questions or possibilities |
| Who determines the next step | Participants choose whether to explore, combine, test, defer or commit |
| What remains after a decision | The chosen direction, alternatives, rationale and unresolved constraints |

### Make the tradeoff real

1. **Preserve productive differences.** Keep competing assumptions visible when they matter. Allow “unresolved” as a useful state; a polished consensus summary is not required to finish a session.
2. **Reveal opportunities to work together.** Link a contribution that could address another contribution's constraint. Show the source evidence and ask whether the connection is meaningful. Do not equate topic similarity with collaboration or assign people automatically.
3. **Offer possibilities without prescribing the answer.** Let participants branch, reframe, combine, reject or defer a suggestion. Support decisions and tasks once the team chooses a direction; do not silently promote tentative discussion into commitment.
4. **Keep operating effort low and reflection deliberate.** Recording, navigation and draft organization stay simple. Invite brief reflection when an assumption is challenged or a consequential direction is selected. Friction in the ideas is material to examine, not extra friction to add to the interface.

Example: a designer asks for a seamless enclosure while an engineer asks for easy battery replacement. Branch could link the two expressed requirements and ask, “How might access remain unobtrusive?” The team may explore a concealed panel, a sliding shell or another approach, then choose what to prototype. The collaboration opportunity is a shared design question. Any suggested solution is clearly a proposal, not something attributed to the speakers.

**The strategic sacrifice:** Branch accepts some explicit review and room for exploration in exchange for participant ownership and better-supported commitments. It targets uncertain concept-development work; a routine status meeting may need only concise notes and actions. It should not prolong a discussion merely to appear creative.

### Strength and limits of the counter-position

This is a stronger strategic position than adding a graph to a notetaker: it changes the audience's main job, the default output, the review workflow and the success criteria. The business value to test is identifying consequential assumptions earlier, finding useful combinations, and avoiding rework from premature decisions. Measure those outcomes alongside review effort and distraction; meeting length, graph size and number of suggestions are inadequate proxies.

It remains a differentiation hypothesis, not a demonstrated structural barrier to competitors adopting similar features. Granola could add exploration tools without abandoning its existing offering. MeetMap is also direct prior art for live collaborative dialogue mapping. Potential defensibility must be earned through trusted interpretation, facilitation quality and useful project history across repeated sessions.

Preserve source-linked evidence, participant correction, access boundaries, optional AI, portable exports and the open-source/community-supported direction. Possible tension is an editable interpretation of expressed contributions, not a diagnosis of interpersonal conflict or employee performance. Optional hosting or team services remain a funding hypothesis.

**Pitch line: Turn differences into possibilities.**

## 4. Autopilot and intentional friction

**Automate capture and reversible organization. Ask for deliberate input when meaning, commitment or access changes.**

Autopilot refers to familiar actions that require little attention; background automation supports them. It does not mean starting a microphone without a deliberate action or permission.

| Moment | Keep effortless | Add intentional friction when |
|---|---|---|
| Start or join a session | Familiar Record / Pause / Stop; remember permitted preferences; show current audience | First use or a changed audience/processing scope needs clear participant awareness and appropriate consent before live capture/AI processing |
| Follow the discussion | Transcribe within allowed scope; draft nodes and possible links; preserve source timestamps | A proposed relationship is promoted to accepted meaning, a contribution is attributed ambiguously, or a statement is marked as an agreed decision |
| Explore and correct | Tap a node for evidence; pan/zoom; undo reversible edits; preserve view position | Merging ideas or resolving a contradiction would erase a meaningful difference; show the effect and retain the prior versions |
| Notice a possible connection | Offer a quiet, source-linked suggestion without interrupting each speaker | The team chooses to adopt the connection, change a constraint or act on a proposed direction |
| Finish and return | Save source material, draft map and history automatically; resume the same project | Briefly review consequential decisions, disputed links and unresolved questions; unreviewed suggestions can remain drafts |
| Share or export | Keep working within the already authorized session audience | Private material enters a shared map, new people gain access, or an export includes sources beyond the current scope |
| Remove material | Offer undo for genuinely reversible operations | Irreversible deletion or a major restore changes evidence/meaning; preview the impact first |

Do not ask for approval on every new node, word, layout adjustment or status update. Group review at natural pauses, when a decision is proposed, or at the session's end. The team should be able to stay in conversation while the map develops.

AI remains optional: manual mapping, source access, editing and export should work without it. Live automated transcription/semantic mapping requires enabled AI and the appropriate input permissions. Turning AI off cannot preserve the same automation promise.

## 5. Accumulating value and earning repeat use

**Every session should make the next session more useful. Branch becomes the team's evolving memory of how its ideas developed, so people can resume, reconnect and build on earlier thinking.**

The value is the reasoning people can recover and reuse, not the number of recordings or nodes stored. A live map provides immediate value; a persistent, participant-reviewed project map is the proposed reason to return.

The value loop is: **capture contributions → connect them to prior thinking → review consequential meaning → reuse it in real work → add the new outcome.** Capture and draft organization stay effortless; review happens at natural pauses or when a decision matters.

| What accumulates | When the team comes back | Value to the team |
|---|---|---|
| Decisions, alternatives and their reasons | A familiar proposal resurfaces or someone asks “why did we choose this?” | Recover the reasoning without replaying an entire meeting or repeating the same debate |
| Unresolved questions and constraints | The next session begins or a constraint changes | Start from what still needs work; distinguish an open question from an agreed decision |
| Connections across sessions | A new contribution relates to an older, shelved idea | Reconsider a possibility with its original context and rejection reason intact |
| A history of revisions and outcomes | A prototype or experiment produces new evidence | Update the map, mark assumptions as changed, and see how the team's direction developed |
| Shared explanations linked to sources | A teammate joins or work changes hands | Understand both the current direction and how the team reached it |

For example, a team shelves a modular enclosure because tooling is too expensive. Weeks later, a different fabrication method is discussed. Branch could suggest a connection to that earlier option and show the cost assumption that ruled it out. The team decides whether the new method actually changes the decision. This is a proposed interaction, not a validated capability.

### Make returning easy

- Open a project to a brief “where we left off” view: current direction, what changed, and a few unresolved questions. Let people enter the map or start the next recording immediately.
- Resurface old material when it helps the present conversation. Show why it is relevant, link to evidence, and allow dismissal or correction. More suggestions should not mean more interruption.
- Preserve stable idea identity and provenance across sessions. Keep AI proposals distinct from accepted meaning; corrections and new evidence can supersede earlier interpretations without erasing history.
- Use only authorized project context. Cross-project reuse follows explicit access boundaries; private reflections are not silently pooled. Better retrieval from a growing project record does not require training a model on team conversations.
- Keep records portable and exportable. Earn retention through usefulness, not difficulty leaving.

### Smallest version and validation

Start with one persistent project map, decision/alternative history, unresolved questions, and a short resume view. Test relevant reconnection suggestions before adding a large organizational knowledge system.

Run the pilot across several sessions on the same real project. Observe voluntary returns by both facilitators and participants, successful retrieval or reuse of earlier reasoning, and whether open questions progress. Also record correction effort, distraction and stale suggestions. Compare with the team's usual notes/whiteboard workflow. The retention hypothesis is that recovered context and useful connections outweigh the effort of reviewing the map; it remains unvalidated.

**Pitch line: The more your team uses Branch, the less it has to reconstruct—and the more earlier thinking it can build on.**

## Applying the attached study accurately

Ben Shaw's *A Cognitive Account of Collective Emergence in Design* presents vignettes of collaborative engineering design. The supplied slides distinguish combinations and transformations of contributions and describe novel outcomes as emerging through contributions layered across people. In the cryobot example, constraints on an extending boom contribute to a new direction: extending upward. See the [supplied presentation](../references/collective-emergence/Collective%20Emergence%20-%20Ben%20Shaw%202009.pdf), especially PDF pages 9–14 (slide labels 51–56).

The product inference is to map **how contributions change and combine**, not merely sort a transcript into topics. The supplied deck is not a controlled evaluation proving that a live AI map increases creativity. It also leaves social dynamics, hierarchy and applicability in other domains as further work.

For the proposed comparison:

- **Within-person development:** changes across the same person's expressed ideas, or explicitly contributed before/after reflections.
- **Across-person development:** how one person's expressed idea relates to another person's challenge, elaboration or combination.

A transcript cannot reveal unspoken thought, and an inferred link does not prove causal influence. Display evidence and uncertainty, let participants correct the interpretation, and avoid ranking creativity or employee performance. Private reflections enter a shared map only by choice.

MeetMap is a close research precedent: a study with 20 participants in ten pairs compared two AI-assisted mapping variants with a baseline. It supports investigating the balance between automation and active sense-making; it does not establish demand or outcomes for Branch's target teams.

## Small next validation step

Run an exploratory pilot with several real design/engineering teams on an actual unresolved concept. Compare their usual notes/whiteboard workflow with Branch on whether participants can accurately explain one another's constraints, trace a changed idea to evidence, recognize an unresolved assumption and resume the discussion later. Record correction effort and distraction from the live map. Check whether the facilitator and the other participants both want to use it again. Set criteria before interpreting the results; do not advertise numerical improvements without data.
